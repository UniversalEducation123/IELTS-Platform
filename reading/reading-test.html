Passage 1: Do animals dream?

Studies using electrodes attached to the heads of sleepers have shown that when we sleep, we do so in two ways that alternate throughout the night. The first is rapid eye movement (REM) or active sleep. During this stage our eyes move, even though our eyelids are closed. Our muscles also twitch slightly, though they are largely paralysed so we don't hurt ourselves. In contrast, we also engage in non-REM sleep, during which we barely move at all. Most dream states, and certainly those with the most vivid dreams, happen during REM sleep.

There's some evidence that other mammals may also dream. For example, researchers compared the brain patterns of rats running through a maze when awake with their brain patterns during REM sleep. They found the patterns were very similar and concluded that the sleeping rats were dreaming about going through the maze.

Researchers also recorded brain activity in sleeping pigeons. As in mammals, the recordings revealed both REM and non-REM sleep. REM sleep activity was high in brain regions involved in processing visual information, especially images related to physical activities such as flying.

Whales and dolphins do not shut down their entire brain when they sleep, but only half of it, keeping the rest awake. They show no sign of REM sleep. It is thought that they don't experience REM sleep because during REM sleep animals are more vulnerable to extremes of temperature.

Growing evidence from birds and mammals suggests that REM sleep and dreaming are important for forming memories and learning. When events are replayed in dreams, this helps integrate memories into longer-term storage.

We still cannot know exactly what animals experience in their dreams. Dogs primarily navigate the world using smell, while spiders rely much more on vibrations. Therefore, their dream experiences may be very different from human dreams.

In 2019, David Scheel observed an octopus named Heidi apparently dreaming. Another octopus called Costello appeared to have a nightmare. However, brain imaging is still needed to prove that octopuses replay activities from their waking lives.

Passage 2: Mapungubwe

Mapungubwe was a kingdom in southern Africa below the Limpopo River. It flourished between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries CE. It was formed by Bantu-speaking farmers. Cattle herding and farming produced food surpluses that could be traded for other goods.

At its peak, the population was around 5,000 people. The king was probably the wealthiest individual and lived with advisers in a stone enclosure on top of a natural sandstone hill. Other people lived in mud and thatch houses below the hill.

Members of the king's family probably lived in grander residences around Babandyanalo. Local chiefs lived in smaller stone residences across the plateau.

Animal remains and ivory suggest trade with coastal areas. Glass beads probably came from India. Trade links helped strengthen political authority.

Pottery was produced on a large scale, suggesting prosperity. Finds included vessels, bowls, ceramic discs, whistles, animal figurines and stylised human figures. Some figures may have been used as offerings to ancestors, although their exact purpose is unknown. Copper jewellery was also discovered.

Gold objects were found at the royal burial site. Gold was made into rectangular sheets with geometric patterns. Similar decoration was found at Great Zimbabwe. Mapungubwe declined partly because of overpopulation, pressure on resources and possible droughts. Trade routes may also have moved northwards.

Passage 3: Artificial Intelligence

In many Western countries, excitement about artificial intelligence is widespread. Media reports suggest that AI may cure diseases, accelerate innovation and improve creativity. This has created a mindset called AI solutionism: the belief that enough data and machine learning can solve all human problems.

This mindset can be dangerous because it creates unrealistic expectations and ignores AI safety principles. Governments are competing to dominate machine learning, but politicians often fail to understand the complexity of introducing advanced systems into the real world.

Neural networks are modelled loosely on the human brain. However, simply adding a neural network does not automatically make a system fair or inclusive. Public organisations often lack suitable data infrastructure. Data may be stored offline, hidden in bureaucracy or spread across departments requiring special permission.

Public organisations also lack people with the skills needed to use machine intelligence effectively. Researchers such as Stuart Russell recommend practical, everyday applications rather than exaggerated predictions. Rodney Brooks argues that AI innovations take much longer to become widely used than people expect.

AI systems are vulnerable to adversarial attacks. One AI may deliberately manipulate another AI into making wrong predictions. Security standards and defence mechanisms are often overlooked.

AI should be applied carefully in government, business and society. People must discuss ethics, distrust and the limitations of AI. Human beings still need to lead in many areas.

IBM Watson for Oncology was designed to help doctors treat cancer, but medical experts found it difficult to trust its recommendations and the programme was abandoned in many hospitals. In the legal system, algorithms used to assess criminal risk were found to amplify structural discrimination and were also abandoned.
